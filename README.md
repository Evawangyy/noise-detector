Noise Detector Project (STC89C52RC)
This repository records my journey of building a noise detector using an 8051 microcontroller. As a high school student, I started from zero, experienced many hardware failures, and learned a lot through troubleshooting.
Here is my development log:
Part 1: The Basic Version (Threshold Alarm)
First, I tried the most simple one. When clapping or coughing, the light turns on and the buzzer sounds.
Finding P1.0: During connecting wires, I couldn't find P1.0 on the board. Later I learned the silkscreen differs from common conventions: to save space, boards often omit the dot and label P1.0 as P10.
Adjusting the Potentiometer: The goal is to turn the potentiometer in a quiet environment until the signal indicator LED just turns off. I turned the cross-head potentiometer counterclockwise with a small screwdriver. However, even after 15 to 20 turns, the switch indicator LED remained on. This made me realize that the threshold adjustment might be in the opposite direction. I then rotated it the other way and successfully turned the LED off.
Downloading the Code: Next, I edited the code in Keil uVision4 and used STC-ISP to download it. It is important to note that you must turn the power switch off and then on again (cold start) for the download to be successful. The original code came from an online video tutorial.
The "Always Ringing" Bug: After completing this, I encountered a problem: the buzzer kept ringing as soon as I turned on the power. I unplugged the DO pin from the KY-037 and kept turning the potentiometer until the indicator LED just turned off. However, the buzzer still rang when I plugged it back in. After asking AI for help, I found that LM393 modules from some manufacturers have a reversed output logic: they output 0 (low level) when quiet and 1 (high level) when there is sound. For this type of module, adjusting until the LED turns off means it is outputting 0, which triggers the alarm. Therefore, I modified the code to if (SoundSensor == 1) {, and it worked! The sound detection device started working normally.
Part 2: The Advanced Version (Reading Values)
To make it more advanced, I decided to create a more complicated noise detector. I just bought some new components, including an ADC0832 (voltage converter), two 100 Ohm resistors, and I am waiting for my LCD1602 screen and the STC89C52RC board.
While waiting for the delivery, I started planning and testing the wiring:
Breadboard Nightmare: To connect the ADC0832 and the sound sensor, the sheer number of wires forced me to route everything through a breadboard. Utilizing the vertical internal connections of the breadboard saved a lot of space, but the debugging process was a nightmare.
Short Circuit & Blank Screen: Initially, the LCD screen refused to light up (solved by replacing faulty jumper wires). Then, a stray female header shorted against a nearby pin, causing the sensor's power to go out (luckily, the MCU survived). After fixing the short, the system crashed with continuous buzzing and a blank screen.
Hardware Failure: After systematically checking every wire, I realized my rotary potentiometer was completely worn out from excessive twisting!
The Workaround: As a quick fix, I bypassed the potentiometer and wired a fixed resistor directly to the LCD to set the contrast voltage. The screen lit up perfectly, proving the circuit and code were actually fine.

Despite the many rebuilds, shorts, and broken components due to my lack of experience, seeing the device finally measure real sound brought me immense joy. It was a painful but incredibly rewarding hardware debugging journey!
